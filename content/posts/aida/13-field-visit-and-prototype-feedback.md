## Summary

The class visits Engestofte Gods, sees the organization in its real setting, and presents project prototypes. The visit connects the original company presentation with a tangible solution and gives the team an opportunity to gather reactions from the people closest to the problem.

Assumptions that seem reasonable in a classroom can fail in the environment where a product will be used. Direct feedback can reveal missing context, misunderstood workflows, practical constraints, and which parts of a prototype create genuine interest or confusion.

## Making the visit useful

The prototype presentation is an opportunity to compare the team's theory of the problem with the company's reality. Useful preparation includes:

- A short explanation of the problem the team believes it is solving.
- A focused demonstration of the main user flow rather than every technical feature.
- Specific questions about terminology, workflow, value, and missing information.
- Notes on what stakeholders do, hesitate over, or misunderstand during the demo.
- A clear distinction between confirmed feedback and the team's interpretation of it.

After the visit, the observations can be translated into project changes:

1. Confirm or revise the problem statement.
2. Update the specification and system sketch.
3. Reprioritize the remaining tickets.
4. Record requests that are valuable but outside the current scope.
5. Explain in the portfolio what changed because of the visit.

## Key takeaways

A prototype is most useful as a conversation tool. Strong feedback is connected to observed behavior and specific workflows, then translated into changes to requirements, priorities, or the system design.

- Demonstrate early enough that major changes are still affordable.
- Ask for examples from actual work instead of abstract approval.
- Look for contradictions between the original brief and current feedback.
- Preserve a short decision log showing how stakeholder input affected the project.

## Closing note

Showing unfinished work can feel uncomfortable, but it is cheaper and more informative than polishing the wrong solution. The visit is a reminder that stakeholder understanding should be refreshed throughout a project, not only during the initial interview.
